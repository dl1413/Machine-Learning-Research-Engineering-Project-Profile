Dear Capacity & Efficiency Team,

I'm applying for the Data Science Engineer role. My background sits at the
intersection you're hiring for: applied statistics with a Bayesian core,
production-oriented ML tooling, and a habit of treating uncertainty and
calibration as first-class quantities rather than optional post-hoc analysis.
I'm an M.S. Applied Statistics candidate at RIT (2026) and my portfolio is
three published case studies, each written to that discipline.

The LLM Ensemble Bias Detection study is the closest analogue to capacity
work — you have many noisy judges, real publisher-level structure, and the
question is how to summarize their agreement without losing the signal in
the disagreement. I evaluated 4,500 textbook passages with a rubric-based LLM
ensemble, produced 67,500 individual ratings, reached Krippendorff's α = 0.84,
and modeled publisher-level effects with Bayesian partial pooling and MCMC
diagnostics. The delivered product was a workflow that hands reviewers
uncertainty rather than false certainty.

The Breast Cancer Classification benchmark is the discipline half. Eight
ensemble models on the WDBC dataset with cross-validation, feature selection,
calibration, and threshold analysis; 99.12% held-out accuracy and 0.9987
ROC-AUC on the best configuration, framed as a benchmark rather than a
clinical claim. It's the kind of careful comparison work that catches an
apples-to-oranges regression before it ships.

The AI Safety Red-Team Evaluation study rounds it out. A two-stage evaluation
across 12,500 response pairs and six harm categories — LLM ensemble labels
plus a supervised classifier — separately reporting α = 0.81 and 96.8% held-out
accuracy so a reader can reason about annotation quality and model performance
independently. PyMC, SHAP, MLflow throughout.

I'd bring careful measurement, honest uncertainty, and enough engineering to
own the pipeline that produces it. Portfolio and PDFs:
https://github.com/dl1413/Machine-Learning-Research-Engineering-Project-Profile
