I'm applying for the Quantitative Researcher, Machine Learning role. My
background is applied statistics with a Bayesian core, and my published case
studies are all built around the same principle Two Sigma research operates
on: a model is worth what its evaluation can defend, not what its headline
metric claims.

The Breast Cancer Classification benchmark is my most direct demonstration
of that discipline in a supervised setting. Eight ensemble methods on the
WDBC dataset — XGBoost, LightGBM, AdaBoost, stacking — evaluated with nested
cross-validation, calibration analysis, threshold selection, and SHAP-based
explainability. 99.12% held-out accuracy and 0.9987 ROC-AUC on the best
configuration, presented alongside the calibration and threshold work rather
than instead of it. The report is explicit that the number is a benchmark
result, not a clinical claim — the framing I would carry into any research
finding.

The LLM Ensemble Bias Detection case study is the closest analogue to noisy
signal aggregation. 4,500 textbook passages, rubric-based LLM judges, 67,500
individual ratings, Krippendorff's α = 0.84, and publisher-level effects
estimated with Bayesian partial pooling and MCMC diagnostics. Convergence
and shrinkage were checked with ArviZ; the delivered artifact was a
disagreement- and uncertainty-aware review pipeline, not a collapsed score.

The AI Safety Red-Team Evaluation study extends the same pattern: a two-stage
evaluation with 12,500 response pairs across six categories, α = 0.81 for
annotator agreement, 96.8% held-out classifier accuracy, Bayesian
hierarchical modeling for risk analysis, kept separate on purpose.
